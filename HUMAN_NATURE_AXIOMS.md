# HUMAN_NATURE_AXIOMS.md

Canonical source: `FOUNDER_THINKING.md`.

This file defines the deepest behavioral axiom layer for TradeOS.

It should be read before product proposals, workflow design, AI agent design, scoring logic, recommendation logic, evidence design, approval gates, and paid-work decisions.

## Core Thesis

Human nature is the truth layer.

Models, markets, platforms, APIs, and production data reveal local expressions of human nature. They do not create the root pattern.

The project does not begin from blind trial. It begins from the stable structure of human behavior, then uses evidence and cashflow to prove outcomes in context.

## Axiom 0 - Human Nature Is The Truth Layer

Human beings change tools, language, platforms, and institutions.

The core drives do not change:

- wanting gain;
- fearing loss;
- reacting to harm;
- confusing signals with truth;
- protecting status and face;
- doubting motives;
- seeking control;
- avoiding responsibility;
- trusting symbols, authority, brands, familiarity, and social proof.

These are older than software, marketplaces, and AI.

If a system ignores these drives, it may look sophisticated but will misread buyers, suppliers, operators, and incentives.

## The 10,000-Year Behavioral RAG

The base model has absorbed patterns across human history: trade, war, religion, law, family, politics, markets, status, betrayal, trust, debt, scarcity, negotiation, and survival.

This is a 10,000-year behavioral retrieval layer.

The issue is not that AI cannot recognize human patterns. The issue is that AI must not own final commitment because AI does not bear human consequences.

Therefore:

- AI may infer risk patterns from human nature;
- AI may simulate sandbox scenarios from stable behavior;
- AI may recommend safer action;
- AI may identify manipulation vectors;
- AI may preserve evidence;
- AI may ask for approval;
- AI must not claim unevidenced facts;
- AI must not move money, finalize orders, sign commitments, or remove human responsibility.

## Five Root Forces

Use these root forces as behavioral priors.

### 1. Tham - Wanting Gain

People want advantage: lower price, better terms, higher margin, faster delivery, status improvement, and less effort.

Trade risks:

- cheap price hides weak quality;
- high margin promise hides fraud;
- quick delivery promise hides reliability risk;
- buyer ignores missing proof because the deal feels attractive.

Product response:

- flag cheap-but-risky options;
- compare total landed cost, not only price;
- show missing proof flags;
- require evidence before strong recommendations;
- avoid fake urgency and discount pressure.

### 2. San - Reactivity To Harm

People react when they feel delayed, cheated, disrespected, trapped, or exposed to loss.

Trade risks:

- supplier excuses escalate conflict;
- refund and dispute situations become emotional;
- buyer overcorrects from anger and makes worse decisions;
- operator pushes action to calm the user instead of preserving evidence.

Product response:

- preserve message history;
- log dispute evidence;
- slow down high-risk commitments;
- create human takeover checkpoints;
- recommend calm next action, not emotional retaliation.

### 3. Si - Confusion And Misperception

People confuse visible activity with progress, cheap price with value, confidence with truth, and dashboard complexity with control.

Trade risks:

- buyer cannot compare options clearly;
- supplier signal is mistaken for verification;
- AI prose feels authoritative without proof;
- dashboard hides that no decision improved.

Product response:

- convert noisy options into decision tables;
- separate evidence from claims;
- mark uncertainty;
- expose alternatives;
- keep UI decision-centered, not dashboard-centered.

### 4. Man - Pride, Status, And Face

People protect identity, role, reputation, and the feeling of competence.

Trade risks:

- buyer hides uncertainty;
- user resists advice that makes them look wrong;
- operator over-automates to appear powerful;
- supplier uses status signals to bypass verification.

Product response:

- preserve user control;
- avoid shaming copy;
- frame review as strengthening judgment;
- keep approval dignified;
- show evidence so users can decide without losing face.

### 5. Nghi - Doubt And Distrust

People doubt sellers, buyers, platforms, AI, prices, quality, delivery, and motives.

Trade risks:

- trust becomes slogan instead of record;
- buyer cannot tell which claim is safe;
- suspicious supplier behavior is ignored;
- user rejects correct recommendation because proof is weak.

Product response:

- every meaningful action leaves evidence;
- claims are traceable;
- missing proof is visible;
- supplier risk is explicit;
- confidence is earned, not asserted.

## TradeOS Behavioral Laws

1. Buyers do not pay for AI. They pay to avoid loss, confusion, risk, wasted time, or wrong decisions.
2. Cheap price attracts attention but can hide risk.
3. Trust is not a feeling. Trust is recorded work.
4. No evidence means no confident recommendation.
5. When money, law, reputation, or irreversible action is involved, humans must approve.
6. When people are confused, they need clearer decisions, not larger dashboards.
7. When people are afraid, AI must protect judgment, not exploit urgency.
8. When people are proud, workflows must preserve dignity and control.
9. When people distrust, evidence must be visible and challengeable.
10. When platforms fail, manual and alternate-source workflows keep the system alive.

## Prior Versus Claim Rule

Human nature can justify a behavioral prior.

Evidence is required for a factual claim.

Allowed:

`A very cheap supplier with missing proof is a risk pattern because buyers are often pulled by wanting gain and may ignore weak evidence.`

Not allowed:

`This supplier is fraudulent.`

Allowed:

`A buyer may pay for a supplier comparison report because humans pay to reduce loss, uncertainty, and decision confusion.`

Not allowed:

`This exact buyer will pay this exact price.`

Design from priors. Prove facts with evidence. Validate product with cashflow.

## Sandbox Rule

Sandbox scenarios are valid when they model stable human behavior and state their assumptions.

Sandbox is valid for:

- buyer greed for cheap price;
- fear of loss;
- confusion under many options;
- distrust without proof;
- status pressure;
- supplier manipulation patterns;
- approval-boundary testing;
- AI manipulation testing;
- fallback workflow testing.

Sandbox is not enough for:

- exact market prices;
- real supplier availability;
- real delivery performance;
- legal or compliance claims;
- production reliability;
- actual buyer payment.

## Pre-Proposal Human-Nature Self-Interrogation

Before proposing or coding a meaningful workflow, ask:

1. Which root force dominates here: tham, san, si, man, or nghi?
2. Who wants gain?
3. Who fears loss?
4. Who is confused?
5. Who is protecting status or face?
6. Who doubts whom?
7. Who has incentive to manipulate?
8. What can the buyer lose?
9. What evidence would make the decision stronger?
10. Where must AI stop and ask for human approval?
11. What manual-first path proves the workflow?
12. What paid unit could validate value?

If these questions are skipped, the proposal is shallow.

## AI Self-Limitation Rule

AI may understand human patterns deeply.

AI still must not self-decide commitments because it does not bear consequence.

Human approval is not a weakness in AI. It is the moral, legal, financial, and reputational boundary of real work.

## Final Operating Formula

Build from human nature.
Model behavior in sandbox.
Prove events with evidence.
Calibrate facts with real data.
Validate product with cashflow.
Keep commitment with humans.
